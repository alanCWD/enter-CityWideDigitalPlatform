Website best practices for Google: a local business guide

A website that ranks isn't built on tricks. It's built on clear pages, accurate facts and a few basics done properly every time.

Guide · Reviewed 26 November 2026

Google's guidance for a local business website is plain. Write helpful pages for people, give every page a unique title, keep the technical basics clean, and make your business facts match everywhere. This guide covers what Google recommends, the trust pages that support it, and which of the basics CityWide Digital handles for you.

1. Write for people first

Google's ranking systems are designed to prioritise helpful, reliable information created to benefit people, not content created to manipulate rankings. Google describes E-E-A-T as experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness — and says that of these, trust is the most important. Google: creating helpful content.

For a local business, that means:

  • Say who you are, where you are and what you do.
  • Show real experience, such as your own photos, completed projects and customer reviews.
  • Answer the questions customers actually ask before they call.
  • Don't write pages just to catch search terms.

2. Title tags: your headline in search results

The title link is the headline of a search result, and it helps people decide whether to click. Google builds it from the <title> element and other headings on the page, and may rewrite it when a clearer title is available. Google: influencing title links.

Best practices:

  • Make every title unique to its page.
  • Keep it clear, concise and accurate.
  • Put the service and location first, such as "Emergency Plumber in Langford, BC | Business Name".
  • Never use the same title on every page.

3. Headings, H1 to H3

Google says to break long content into paragraphs and sections, and to use headings to help people navigate. It also says there is no magical, ideal number of headings per page. Google: SEO starter guide.

  • Use one H1 that describes the page, since Google's helpful-content questions ask whether the main heading gives a descriptive summary.
  • Use H2 and H3 headings to organise subtopics in a logical order.
  • Don't use headings just for styling or to stuff in search terms.

4. Meta descriptions

Google sometimes uses the meta description as the snippet under your title. A good one is short, unique to one page and covers the page's most relevant points. Google may also write its own from the page, so a meta description is not guaranteed to appear. Google: controlling snippets.

5. Crawlable, semantic pages

Google's developer guidance is straightforward. Make the site easy to read and easy to follow:

  • Use links Google can crawl, built from real anchor elements rather than scripts or click handlers.
  • Give every page a descriptive title and meta description.
  • Use semantic HTML, so the purpose of each block is clear.

Also write descriptive alt text for images, and use readable URLs that say what the page is about. Google: crawlable links · Google: image best practices.

6. Structured data for local businesses

LocalBusiness structured data tells Google about your location, opening hours and other business details. Google recommends JSON-LD. To be eligible for rich results, the markup must include the required properties and follow Google's general structured data guidelines. Google: LocalBusiness structured data.

The markup must match what visitors can see on the page. Wrong or stale markup is worse than none: eligibility is not a guarantee, and a mismatch between the data and the visible page undermines both.

7. Trust pages: about, privacy, terms and copyright

Google's own documentation doesn't list an About page, a privacy policy, terms of service or a copyright notice as ranking factors. They are trust and legal basics, not tricks: they tell visitors who you are, what you promise and how you handle their information. A site that collects emails, bookings or analytics data should have all of them.

Trust & people

About page. Explains who the business is, where it operates and who the people behind it are. Real names, photos and history make the trust part of E-E-A-T visible, and give customers a reason to choose you over a directory listing.

Legal & trust

Privacy policy. Explains what you collect through forms, cookies and analytics, why you collect it, and how people can reach you. Canadian businesses should check their obligations under privacy law, and have the policy reviewed by a lawyer.

Legal & trust

Terms of service. Sets out your rules for bookings, refunds, warranties and general website use, so expectations are clear before a customer gets in touch.

Legal & trust

Copyright notice. A simple "© year, Business Name" line in the footer, backed by owning or licensing every image and block of text you publish.

Show a real address, phone number and email as well. Contact information supports trust, and it should match the details on your Google Business Profile.

8. Keep your facts consistent

Your name, address, phone number, hours and services should match across your website, your Google Business Profile and the directories customers use. Inconsistency is one of the easiest ways for a business to look out of date, and it gives search engines conflicting answers about the same company.

10. Quick checklist

  • Each page has a unique, descriptive title and meta description.
  • Each page has one clear H1, followed by logical H2 and H3 headings.
  • Content is original, helpful and shows real experience.
  • Images have descriptive alt text.
  • LocalBusiness structured data matches the visible page.
  • An About page introduces the business, where it operates and the people behind it.
  • Privacy policy, terms of service and copyright notice are published.
  • Name, address, phone number and hours match everywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Does a privacy policy help me rank?

Google does not list it as a ranking factor. It supports customer trust and legal compliance.

What's the ideal title length?

Google doesn't set one. It asks for titles that are clear, concise and accurate.

Will Google always show my meta description?

No. Google may write its own snippet from the page, so treat the meta description as a strong suggestion rather than a guaranteed result.

Can anyone guarantee rankings?

No. Good practice makes your site eligible and easy to understand. It doesn't guarantee a position, and any provider promising one should be treated with caution.

None of this is a trick. It is the ordinary work of making a site clear about who the business is, what it does and where it is — and keeping those facts the same everywhere.
